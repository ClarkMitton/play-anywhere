# WWW: What Went Well

A running log, one entry per lesson built for the Immersive Learning Room.
Newest first. Each entry has two halves:

- **In the build**: what worked when turning a teacher's material into a lesson. Filled in when the lesson is made.
- **In the room**: what worked with real learners. Filled in by whoever taught it, after the session.

"Even better if" sits next to each so the next lesson starts from what we learned.

What proves itself here is collected in [WAYS-OF-WORKING.md](WAYS-OF-WORKING.md).

## The Five Parties: Who Wins Your Vote?

- **Built:** 19 October 2026
- **For:** Level 2 citizenship, 15 learners aged 16 to 18, last lesson on a Friday
- **Length:** 120 minutes with a 10 minute break, 31 steps, three whole-class moves
- **Source:** the teacher's Canva deck (8 slides, "The Five Largest Political Parties in the UK Parliament") and their Canva comparison page (healthcare, education, immigration and public spending for each party), plus five learning objectives they chose
- **Lesson file:** [five-uk-parties.sql](five-uk-parties.sql)
- **Slides:** the 8 Canva slides, 4 policy cards and 2 leader slides, in `public/lessons/five-uk-parties/` and the media storage
- **Table packs:** none. Whiteboard tables yes, no printing.

### WWW in the build

- **Facts were checked before building.** Labour had a new leader the slides did not show: Andy Burnham has been Prime Minister since July. The leaders were confirmed against Parliament's members list, and the Greens' "variable" seats became 5 MPs.
- **The leaders use free photos.** Four are Parliament's official portraits (CC BY 3.0, credited on the slide). Zack Polanski is not an MP, so his photo is a CC0 one from Wikimedia Commons.
- **A Canva site that cannot be embedded became picture cards.** Canva blocks its sites from being shown inside other pages, and the app no longer has the proxy that got round that. Its four rows were rebuilt word for word as portrait cards for the touch screens, in the deck's colours.
- **The touch screens are a reference library during table work.** While the rotation timer runs on the big screen, the touch screens show the spending card and one runner per table goes to read it.
- **Learners choose the local issue.** A word cloud and a vote on what matters to 16 to 18 year olds in Bradford set what each table works on after the break.
- **Assigned parties keep it balanced.** A wheel gives each table its party, so nobody argues for their own politics, and the pitch vote judges how well a pitch fits its voter, not the party.
- **Checked before anyone runs it.** All 31 steps passed the app's content rules, the SQL ran against a real database engine, and all 14 pictures load from the media storage.

### Even better if (build)

- Steps 7 to 11 are five party slides in a row, held together by one watching task. Watch the pace.
- The leader facts will go out of date fast: the Green leader was standing in a by-election. Check them the day before.
- With 15 learners at two touch screens, about eight people share each screen for the research sprints. Have the ones at the front read aloud.

## Salon: Who Keeps Us Safe? (Hayley, session 2)

- **Built:** 15 October 2026
- **For:** Hayley, Level 1 Hairdressing (16 to 19), session 2 of the health and safety unit (VTCT UV10477, Follow health and safety in the salon)
- **Length:** 60 minutes, 24 steps, about 35 minutes at the whiteboard tables, two whole-class moves
- **Source:** `Hayley/HS Acts.ppt`, `Hayley/Health and safety hazards and hygiene.pptx`, `Hayley/health and safety fill in the gaps handout.doc`, `Hayley/health Safety Acts Handout.docx`, the UV10477 unit specification, and two YouTube clips (BBC London, "Could your barber be putting you at risk?", 2:50; Christian Weaver, "The Law in 60 Seconds: Health and Safety at Work", 1:33)
- **Lesson file:** [salon-who-keeps-us-safe.sql](salon-who-keeps-us-safe.sql)
- **Slides:** 14, rebuilt in Canva ("Who Keeps the Salon Safe?" in Clark's Canva), in `public/lessons/salon-who-keeps-us-safe/` and the media storage
- **Table packs:** none. Clark asked for whiteboard tables and no printing.

### WWW in the build

- **Built on what session 1 had already done.** Session 1's transcript was read first, then mapped against the unit specification. It had covered hazards, hazard and risk, five laws and COSHH. This session fills the gaps: the employer's responsibilities (1f, on the external paper, never touched), safe and hygienic working (1c, 1g), and four laws not yet met (Workplace Regulations, Manual Handling, PUWER, Environmental Protection Act). Fire and emergencies are saved for session 3.
- **The brief was agreed before building.** Clark chose the thread (who keeps the salon safe on day one, the employer or me), the product (a Day One induction board), three objectives, Canva slides and no printing.
- **One line down the table holds the lesson together.** Learners split their table into EMPLOYER and ME at the start and fill both sides all lesson. The final board is built from it.
- **Hayley's slides kept their shape.** Her "the employer must… what are your duties? / Let's check" pattern became predict-then-check on the tables. Her fill-the-gaps sheet became a timed race on screen.
- **A real case opens and closes the judging.** Tables judge the BBC barbershop before any teaching, vote again with the law behind them, then judge another table's board as inspectors.
- **Both transcripts were read before use.** The caption capture trick from ESOL Adjectives no longer worked, so the player's own caption request was intercepted inside the page instead.
- **Checked before anyone runs it.** All 24 steps passed the app's content rules, the SQL ran against a real database engine, and every slide loads from the media storage.

### Even better if (build)

- Canva's AI rewrote nearly every slide when generating the deck. It swapped in different laws (including the Fire Safety Order, which belongs in session 3), replaced the gap-fill and changed the board task. Every text box was put back by hand. Next time, check the wording before looking at the design.
- Slide body text is about 27 to 34 pixels on a 1920 wide slide. Check it can be read from the back of the room.
- The BBC clip has a first-hand description of an infection and photos of dirty razors. Fine for this group, but warn them.
- Steps 12 to 17 are six slides in a row. Each has a different job (thumbs up, predict the bin, check your partner), but watch the pace.
- The board and judging timers move on by themselves. A tutor who wants more time has to pause.

## ESOL Adjectives

- **Built:** 13 October 2026
- **For:** Shamim Nizami, ESOL Entry 2, typical group of 16
- **Length:** 60 minutes, 24 steps, about 30 minutes writing on the whiteboard tables, two whole-class moves
- **Source:** `Shamim/E2/Adjectives/adjectives.ppt` (14 slides) and one YouTube video (Ellii, "Adjectives", 4 minutes 18 seconds)
- **Lesson file:** [esol-adjectives.sql](esol-adjectives.sql)
- **Slides:** Shamim's slides rebuilt as ten pictures, one slide for the video's two patterns, and five Canva photos, all in `public/lessons/esol-adjectives/`
- **Table packs:** none. Clark asked for no printing, only writing on the tables and the side screens.

### WWW in the build

- **The brief was agreed before building.** Clark chose the thread (describing people, then where the adjective goes), three objectives, the final product (three sentences about a classmate) and the slide style from a short set of options. Tables and printing were stated up front.
- **The PowerPoint is the spine, in Shamim's order.** Every sentence slide is in the lesson with her wording. Her worksheet tasks became table tasks: "do the worksheet" is three plain sentences to improve on the table, and "think about your classmates" is the final product.
- **Her clip art was recovered.** The old `.ppt` holds its pictures as Windows metafiles. They were pulled out and redrawn at full size, so eight of her own pictures are on the slides.
- **The whole transcript was read this time.** YouTube's transcript panel would not load, but the captions the player fetched could be read from the browser. The practice section has no speech, so its five mixed-up sentences were read from still frames.
- **One video, two jobs.** The first 2 minutes 31 seconds teach the two patterns with a watching task. The video's own practice section then plays as a separate step and becomes a race at the tables.
- **Write first, then see hers.** Four times learners describe a picture before Shamim's sentence appears, each in a different way: a two minute list, a one minute race, one spoken word, a table guess.
- **Canva made photos with no words.** Asking for pictures only, and drawing the words here, meant nothing garbled to fix and no credits spent on text slides.
- **Slides checked as a set.** Twelve people across five photos: a mix of ages, ethnicities and body types, a hijab, a wheelchair user and a child. Slide 5 was made to match Shamim's four named people.
- **Two moves in sixty minutes.** Tables, one trip to the touch screens for a question round and a word cloud, then back. The word cloud doubles as the word bank for the last task. One runner per table types the final sentence.
- **Mixed question shapes:** true or false, odd one out, and a best-answer question where clever and thoughtful both look right.
- **Checked before anyone runs it.** All 24 steps passed the app's content rules and the SQL was run against a real database engine.

### Even better if (build)

- Every picture was broken when Clark first opened the lesson, as in every lesson before it. The lesson pointed at files inside the app, which only exist after a publish. The pictures for all five lessons are now in the app's media storage and the lessons point there. `fix-picture-links.sql` repoints anything already in the database.
- 24 steps is a lot. Ten are one or two minute slides, but watch whether the first twenty minutes feel like a slide show.
- Slides 3 and 4 share one screen, and slides 6, 7 and 8 share one screen, to keep the step count down. Shamim may prefer one slide each.
- Her clip art of the lady (a showgirl in a leotard) was swapped for a photo. Worth telling her.
- "Sort and justify" (positive and negative) was offered as an objective and not chosen. It survives as a thumbs up or down check and one question, because the last task needs positive words.
- The practice clips give about ten seconds per sentence, too fast to write. The tutor has to pause the video by hand. A pause-on-cue feature would fix this.
- The timer still hides the slide, so both table tasks rely on the tutor writing on the board first.
- In the Canva photo of the classmates the woman asked for as "in her fifties" looks about thirty. Ages are the thing the AI drifts on.
- The photos could only be downloaded by placing them in a holder deck in Canva and exporting it. That deck ("ESOL Adjectives - picture stills") is still in Clark's Canva.
- The shared board (`padlet`) is still missing from the app's content rules, so that step was not checked by them.

## ESOL This Is Me

- **Built:** 12 October 2026
- **For:** Shamim Nizami, ESOL Pre-Entry (week 2)
- **Length:** 60 minutes, 19 steps, about 27 minutes writing or drawing on the whiteboard tables, four whole-class moves
- **Source:** `Shamim/week 2/` (alphabet Wordwall, a 49 second audio track, two YouTube clips, the homework note)
- **Lesson file:** [esol-this-is-me.sql](esol-this-is-me.sql)
- **Slides:** five, made in Canva, in `public/lessons/esol-this-is-me/`
- **Audio:** `public/lessons/esol-this-is-me/names-and-numbers.mp3`
- **Table packs:** none. Clark asked for no printing, so everything is written or drawn on the tables.

### WWW in the build

- **No printing at all.** The first build had an envelope with a model card and picture cards. Clark asked for writing on the table with activities instead, so the tutor writes their own card on the board as the model, and the picture cards became "draw one, your table says it". A number dictation on the table was added too.
- **The brief was agreed before building.** Clark chose the thread (this is me, and my morning), three objectives and the final product from a short set of options.
- **The app can now play sound.** There was no audio activity, so one was added: "Audio (listening)". It plays on the big screen only, when the tutor presses Play, with Pause and Play again, and never moves the lesson on. The first version started by itself; Clark asked for a Play button, because an activity needs explaining first. A class needs to hear a track more than once, and the tutor decides when.
- **Two topics, one thread.** Introductions and morning routine are different lessons, so both feed one About me card: name, country, three morning things. The card is then used with a partner from another table.
- **The lesson follows the videos.** YouTube's chapter marks showed the first clip runs names, age, where from, jobs, so it stops at 1:55 before jobs. The morning clip's own description lists its eleven sentences and says the second half is speak-along, so learners act out the first half and speak the second.
- **Nobody reads to take part.** Hello before any teaching, fingers before the numbers slide, the tutor acting out the morning before the words appear, actions during the video, hands high or rocking for always and sometimes.
- **The opening question is the closing question.** "Hello! What's your name?" gets one word in minute one and three sentences in minute sixty.
- **Learners waiting at a touch screen have a job:** ask the person next to them where they are from.
- **A runner types the product.** The partner's name and country go on the shared board, which tests "How do you spell it?" for real.
- **Slides checked as a set.** Ten adults across five slides, with a mix of ages, ethnicities, faith dress, body types and a wheelchair user. One photo was not suitable for the room and was replaced.

### Even better if (build)

- The SQL was handed over before the commits were pushed, so Clark opened the lesson and every Canva slide was blank. Push first, then hand over.
- The Wordwall was first put on the big screen with the tutor tapping. Clark corrected this: a Wordwall is an activity and always goes on the touch screens. Moving it added a trip to the screens, so the lesson now has four whole-class moves, the first straight from the hello walk.
- The audio could not be listened to while building, so the lesson does not hold its answers. The tutor needs to listen first and note the names and numbers.
- The first video's transcript could not be read, only its chapter marks. The watching task asks for one name, one number and one place, which works whatever the speakers say, but the cut at 1:55 needs checking by eye.
- A model card slide (Amina's card) was tried before the last task and removed at Clark's request: it did not work, because the slide goes when the timer starts. A timer that could sit on top of a slide would fix this.
- The Wordwall was opened as far as its start screen ("Open the box"). What is in the boxes was not checked.
- "Say and spell my name" was offered as an objective and not chosen. Spelling is still in the lesson as one of the three questions, but it gets four minutes, not ten.
- The shared board (`padlet`) is drawn by the app but is missing from the app's content rules, so those two steps were not checked by the rules. It was the same in the earlier lessons.
- The audio is a publisher's track (Headway Beginner workbook) and now sits in the repo. Clark approved this. Worth confirming the college's licence covers it.
- Slide 5 has seven dots for always and eight for sometimes. It reads fine from a distance but is not tidy.
- The replaced shower picture is a drawing among photos. A photo with no person in it would match better.

## Phantom of the Opera: Escape the Theatre

- **Built:** 8 October 2026 (a demo of what the room can do)
- **For:** ESOL Entry 2, typical group of 16
- **Length:** 45 minutes, 15 steps, 28 minutes at the whiteboard tables or in rotation rounds, two whole-class moves
- **Source:** none; built from a request for a cinematic, escape-room style lesson
- **Lesson file:** [phantom-of-the-opera.sql](phantom-of-the-opera.sql)
- **Slides:** five, made in Canva, in `public/lessons/phantom-of-the-opera/`
- **Table packs:** `public/lessons/phantom-of-the-opera/printables.html`

### WWW in the build

- **The brief was agreed before building.** Length, language focus (reading clues and following instructions), final product (solve the last riddle) and three objectives came from Clark first. Clark skipped the suggested fourth objective at analyse level, so the lesson stops at apply.
- **An escape room without a lock feature.** The app cannot lock a door, so the locks are the printed envelopes: each puzzle gives a code word, and the last riddle only works with both. Learners type the answer to a shared board.
- **One idea:** reading short clues and following instructions. The three puzzles each need a different skill: telling an order from a fact, sequencing with First, Next, Then, Finally, and following place words on a map.
- **Act it out before being told.** The tutor gives four orders aloud, learners do them, and only then see the words on the screen.
- **Clips do real jobs.** The trailer is the hook on all three screens, the Music of the Night clip covers the walk to the touch screens, and the chandelier crash is the finale. Nobody watches two clips in a row.
- **Two moves, not four.** Tables, one trip to the touch screens for a question round and a drawing, then back to the tables. One runner per table types the final answer.
- **Mixed question shapes** in the guard's round: best answer, true or false, best answer.
- **Every table task has a stretch:** write your own order, write directions to Box Five, write a riddle for another table.
- **Slides in Canva, video from YouTube.** Canva AI made the stills (letter, order words, place words, code word, falling chandelier). Clark chose not to spend credits on AI video, so the clips are real YouTube recordings.
- **Checked against the app's own content rules** before anyone runs it. All 15 steps passed.

### Even better if (build)

- The three YouTube links came from a search and were not played end to end. Watch each one, check the sound, and move the trim points (`&end=` and `&start=`) if they cut badly.
- There is no way to lock a step until a code is entered. A real escape room would use one. For now the tutor is the lock.
- The padlet step is a shared board, so the tutor checks answers by eye. A single correct-answer check on screen would be better.
- No confidence check at the start, so the end one has nothing to compare with. Adding one costs a screen trip.
- The Canva deck came back with no garbled words and the exact wording asked for. Slide 2 shows a woman in a hijab, an older Black man, a South Asian man and a white woman who uses a wheelchair. The other four slides have no people, so across the deck there are two women and two men and no children or young adults. Worth asking for a younger face next time.
- On the last slide the red line "Time is running out" is dark red on black and hard to read from the back. On the place-words slide the key is beside the mirror, not clearly behind it, and the text colours split oddly ("NEXT" gold, "TO" white).
- The pack cards are plain parchment. A designer could make them feel more like props: torn programmes, a wax seal, an aged map.

## Us vs AI

- **Built:** 6 October 2026 (first design), rebuilt the same day (second design)
- **For:** Level 2 and Level 3 learners, any subject, typical group of 16
- **Length:** 45 minutes, 20 steps, 17 minutes of group tasks at the whiteboard tables, two whole-class moves
- **Source:** `USvsAI/AI Introductions.txt`
- **Lesson file:** [us-vs-ai.sql](us-vs-ai.sql)
- **Slides:** eight, made in Canva, in `public/lessons/us-vs-ai/`
- **Table packs:** `public/lessons/us-vs-ai/printables.html`

### What the first design got wrong

The first version was 21 steps of video, slide, quiz, repeat. Its outcomes stopped at
"describe" and "identify", its longest task was four minutes, most of the class watched
two touch screens, and "Demonstrate" was a recall quiz. It also tried to use all three
clips, which were really three different lessons. It was thrown away. Everything below
is about the second design.

### WWW in the build

- **The objectives were chosen first, by the person who asked for the lesson.** Four, climbing from identify to explain to analyse to rewrite. Everything else was built to serve them.
- **One idea runs through it:** AI is not thinking, it is predicting. Two of the three clips did not serve that idea and were dropped.
- **The hook opens a question the last task answers.** The sandwich clip asks "was the AI wrong, or was the instruction?" Nobody answers it until learners have rewritten an instruction themselves.
- **Learners do it before they are told.** They finish a sentence as a word cloud, and only then learn that this is all a chatbot does. They draw half past three, and only then see the AI's watches.
- **Two long tasks at the tables.** A case file to analyse and an instruction to rewrite, each with a second round where tables move and test another table's work.
- **The printed packs feel like something.** Cards to sort, a stamped case file with evidence, a black role card that says "You are the AI". Nothing is written on the paper, so they can be reused.
- **Real slides, made in Canva.** A precise brief (read from across a room, few words, no sci-fi, what each picture must show) gave eight usable slides first time.
- **No activity twice in a row,** and the two question rounds have different shapes: quick true or false, then a best-answer question.
- **Every tutor note names its technique,** so the member of staff leaves with approaches as well as a lesson.
- **Works with any number of tables.** One pack per table, and the notes say what to do with only one or two.
- **The video has a watching task.** The tutor sets three questions before the clip, learners write answers on the table as it plays, the same questions sit large on the touch screens, and the next slide opens the floor to them. The first attempt had one small-print question that nobody could read.
- **Two moves, not four.** After a first attempt that sent the whole class to the touch screens for a single vote, the steps were reordered by where learners stand: tables, one trip to the screens for seven steps in a row, then back to the tables. The final answer is typed by one runner per table.

### Even better if (build)

- 20 steps is still more than the dozen we aimed for. Eight of them are one-minute slides, but it is worth watching whether it feels busy.
- One Canva picture misspells "Recommended". It is flagged in the tutor notes as a spot-the-AI-mistake moment, but it should be a choice, not an accident.
- The watch pictures were asked to show ten past ten, so they illustrate the point without proving it. The AI picture tool was unavailable when we tried to run the real test.
- The people in the slides are not a fair mix: four young men, three of them white, and no women. The brief asked for "a young person" and left the rest to the AI. Future briefs name who should appear on each slide, and the deck is checked as a set.
- There is no way yet to get what is written on a table onto the big screen. Learners type their final instruction instead.
- The app has no sorting activity, so the card sort is paper only.

## ESOL Proper Nouns

- **Built:** 6 October 2026
- **For:** Shamim Nizami, ESOL (Entry level)
- **Length:** 60 minutes, 29 steps
- **Source:** `Shamim/Proper nouns.ppt` (15 slides) and three YouTube clips
- **Lesson file:** [esol-proper-nouns.sql](esol-proper-nouns.sql)
- **Pictures:** `public/lessons/esol-proper-nouns/`

### WWW in the build

- **The teacher's slides stayed in the teacher's order.** All 15 slides are in the lesson, with her wording. Nothing was reordered, so she will recognise her own lesson.
- **The original pictures were kept, shown small.** The clip-art is only about 200 pixels wide. Laid out as small labelled cards it stays sharp. Stretched to fill the big screen it would have been a blur.
- **The videos are spread out.** Each one is followed by slides or a touch-screen activity on the same idea. Nobody watches two clips in a row.
- **A long video was cut to the part that matters.** One link was a 54 minute course. The lesson plays only its first chapter (7 minutes 40 seconds) and then stops, by adding `&end=460` to the link. This now works for any lesson.
- **During a video the touch screens carry the task**, for example "Write down the proper nouns you hear", instead of three copies of the same clip playing out of step.
- **The right tool for capital letters.** The word cloud turns every word into small letters. That is perfect for common nouns and wrong for proper nouns, so proper nouns go on the shared board, which shows words exactly as typed.
- **Answer slides after each set of sentences.** The original slides had the sentences but no answers. Learners now mark their own work straight away.
- **The practice gets harder.** Two sets with support, then a final set done alone as the check on learning.
- **Confidence is asked at the start and the end**, so the class sees its own progress on the big screen.
- **Entry-level reading rules held.** Three options at most on every question, short everyday words, faces instead of numbers for confidence.
- **The lesson was tested before anyone ran it.** The SQL was run against a real database engine and every screen was checked against the app's own content rules.

### Even better if (build)

- The first two video links sent were the same clip, so the lesson was built once with a duplicate and then reworked when the right link arrived. Checking the links are all different before building would have saved a rebuild.
- The 8 minute video is long for Entry level learners. Worth watching for attention dropping, and pausing halfway to ask a question.
- An old `.ppt` cannot be opened without PowerPoint, so the slides were rebuilt from their text and pictures. A `.pptx` or a PDF export would let us use the slides exactly as designed.
- 60 minutes is tight with 29 steps. The sentence-writing steps are the ones to trim.
- Two spellings in the source were corrected ("egpyt", "spinx"). Worth telling the teacher, in case they were deliberate.

## Fact and Opinion: Tina Turner (Fiona, Functional Skills Level 1)

- **Built:** 22 October 2026
- **For:** Functional Skills Level 1 English, Fiona's group. Fiona is new to the room and to digital tools, so the build keeps her own slides and adds only what helps.
- **Length:** about 151 minutes as timed, 34 steps (adjust to the real session)
- **Source:** Fiona's PowerPoint (`FIONA FRIDAY FACT AND OPINION 25th October.pptx`, slides from her PDF), her Wordwall, Tina Turner clip and Forms
- **Lesson file:** [fiona-fact-and-opinion.sql](fiona-fact-and-opinion.sql)
- **Pictures:** `lesson-media/lessons/fiona-fact-opinion/` (copies in `public/lessons/fiona-fact-opinion/`)
